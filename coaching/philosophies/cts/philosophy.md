# CTS Coaching Philosophy

## Scope And Status

This file is our documented interpretation of CTS ultrarunning coaching material, especially work associated with Jason Koop. It is not an official CTS document and does not claim to describe every CTS coach, service, or current training plan.

The profile is available for card authoring. It establishes the decision logic that appears consistently in the available source material. Exact workout prescriptions, numerical ranges, and claims that require a closer reading of primary books remain subject to review before being adopted into cards.

## Philosophy In Brief

CTS, as interpreted here, is an event-demand-led ultrarunning system. It aims to build the most useful capacity for the runner's target event through patient workload development, clear intensity definitions, deliberate long-range planning, and carefully chosen specific work.

It is not a philosophy of simply accumulating the largest possible mileage total, nor is it a philosophy of adding hard workouts indiscriminately. The central coaching task is to identify the demands that matter most, develop the runner's limiting capacities in an appropriate order, and place the work where it produces more benefit than cost.

## Coaching Principles

This section describes the high-level decisions that distinguish the CTS interpretation from a generic collection of running advice. These principles are not a fixed weekly program. They are the order of reasoning a coach uses when deciding what matters for an athlete, a goal event, and a longer training cycle.

### Start With The Demands Of The Goal

CTS begins with the event rather than with a standard weekly template. Distance, duration, climbing and descending, surface, altitude, heat, technicality, fueling demands, cutoff pressure, and the runner's own strengths and weaknesses can all change what preparation is most valuable. A card shaped by CTS should make clear which demand it is addressing and why that demand matters. In practice, this means asking what will limit performance on this event and for this runner, then selecting training that changes that limitation. A hard workout is not automatically useful merely because it looks race-specific; it needs a credible connection to the performance problem it is meant to solve.

The goal is not to create a perfect replica of the race in training. It is to identify which demands have the greatest likely consequence and make sure the runner is no longer surprised by them. A flat, fast 50 km race, a steep 100-mile mountain race, and a technical skyrace may all be called trail ultras, but the CTS interpretation would not begin their preparation from the same assumptions.

### Build A Large View Before A Detailed Plan

The long-range plan is a season-level map, not a rigid calendar of daily workouts. It gives purpose to changes in workload, intensity, vertical exposure, nutrition practice, recovery, and race preparation. This philosophy prefers a coherent direction over a sequence of individually attractive sessions that do not add up to a clear preparation. The large view also makes trade-offs visible: adding a new quality block, a high-volume period, or an extra race must displace or change something else. It is therefore a tool for deciding what not to do as much as for deciding what to add.

This long view should remain flexible when life or racing plans change, but its flexibility is not randomness. It gives the coach a reference point for deciding whether a change still serves the season's priorities or merely reacts to the latest good or bad week. The athlete can then understand why a seemingly useful session is being postponed, simplified, or excluded.

For cards, this means a CTS-labelled macro or mezzo card should make the decision sequence visible. The card should identify the event demand, the current limiter, the reason this block comes now, and the trade-off it creates. A plan that adds a quality block, bigger long run, heat exposure, or race rehearsal without naming what it displaces is not really using the long-range view.

### Prioritise The Fundamentals

Durable workload, adequate recovery, consistent training frequency, and the ability to handle work over months and years come before novelty and marginal gains. Advanced tools are not rejected, but they are earned by a sufficient base of training, experience, resilience, and self-awareness. The system is wary of using altitude, heat, complicated workouts, or concentrated training blocks as substitutes for a missing foundation. When progress stalls, the first question is not which advanced intervention to add; it is whether the runner has made the most of sustainable volume, recovery, consistency, and clear execution of the work already in the plan.

This principle is especially relevant for athletes who feel that a plateau proves they need more complexity. CTS instead asks whether the apparent plateau comes from insufficient time at a productive workload, inconsistent training, poor recovery, or a mismatch between the intended and executed intensity. A more sophisticated intervention only helps when it solves a problem the fundamentals cannot solve.

### Use Intensity With Purpose

CTS uses a defined vocabulary for recovery running, endurance running, steady running, tempo running, VO2-max-oriented intervals, and strides. The point is not the terminology itself; it is to make the intended physiological and practical stimulus clear enough that the athlete and coach can execute and review it consistently. Hard work has a place, including work above threshold, but it is a targeted component inside a training year that remains dominated by lower-intensity work. A CTS interpretation should therefore resist the common habit of calling every moderately difficult run "tempo" or treating any breathless interval set as interchangeable. The intended intensity, duration, recovery, terrain, and accumulated fatigue all determine what the session is actually doing.

Clear intensity categories also let the coach see whether a plan is becoming accidentally too hard. If endurance running repeatedly drifts into a harder range, the runner may lose the ability to absorb the deliberately scheduled quality work. If an interval session is completed at an effort that does not match its intended quality, the correct response is to understand why before adding more repetitions or more pace.

### Focus Training When Focus Is Useful

CTS often favours block-style work, where a narrow intensity range or quality receives attention for several weeks, rather than trying to improve every quality in every week. This is especially relevant for experienced runners, runners who have stopped progressing, or runners with limited time. It is a preference for concentrated adaptation, not a rule that every runner or every season must use blocks. The relevant question is whether a focused exposure will address a meaningful limiter without compromising the work that must still be maintained. A block must have an entry point, a defined purpose, an exit, and enough recovery to reveal whether the runner actually adapted.

A focused block deliberately accepts that some capacities may only be maintained while another receives greater attention. That is not a failure if the temporary trade-off is compatible with the long-range plan and event demands. The principle becomes harmful only when concentration is mistaken for permanent specialisation or when the runner continues adding focus after the intended adaptation has already been obtained.

### Respect The Cost Of Training

The correct amount of training is not defined only by total hours, distance, elevation, or a single workload score. The schedule must account for the density of training, the ability to recover, life stress, musculoskeletal cost, and the runner's recent response. Moving, splitting, or consolidating training can preserve one number while changing the actual stimulus and recovery demand. This matters most when a plan is adjusted around work, travel, or family obligations: preserving a weekly total is not automatically equivalent to preserving the intended training. CTS-style decision-making considers continuity, spacing, and the opportunity to recover before assuming that a rearranged week is unchanged.

This protects the runner from a common planning error: treating every training input as interchangeable because the totals look similar. Two hours of continuous running, two separated one-hour runs, and a long run placed after a demanding workday can all have different effects even when the watch records the same duration. The coach must decide which version best serves the current purpose instead of trying to force the original plan into every circumstance.

### Individualise From A Grounded Starting Point

CTS material commonly starts with established practice, then adjusts it to the athlete and event. Individualisation is not permission to invent a different plan every week without logic. It means using the runner's history, goals, tolerance, preferences, feedback, and real-world constraints to decide how the underlying training principles should be applied. The plan remains accountable to a clear hypothesis: what capacity is being developed, what dose is appropriate now, and what observation would justify progressing, maintaining, reducing, or changing it.

A runner's preference is relevant, but it does not automatically overrule the demands of the event or the cost of the work. Equally, an apparently ideal textbook session may be a poor choice if it ignores the athlete's available terrain, prior training, recovery, or motivation. CTS individualisation is therefore a disciplined adaptation of a method, not a collection of personal exceptions with no shared logic.

### Prepare The Whole Ultramarathon Problem

CTS does not treat fitness as the only determinant of race performance. Fueling and hydration practice, environmental preparation, strength where useful, mental skills, logistics, recovery, and an athlete's support system can matter. The practical implication is that relevant non-running demands should be rehearsed in training rather than left for race day. This does not mean every long run becomes a full race simulation. It means the plan deliberately creates enough opportunities to learn about the runner's fueling, equipment, pacing, terrain tolerance, and decision-making before those details become consequential under race pressure.

The wider preparation is still organised around the race rather than becoming a list of wellness tasks. If a variable has little relevance to the runner's goal, it does not need equal attention. If it is likely to decide whether the athlete can keep moving late in the event, it deserves a place in training even when it is not a conventional running workout.

The rehearsal should also create usable information. A fueling practice, descent exposure, heat session, or long outing should leave the coach with a clearer next decision: repeat, progress, simplify, change the strategy, or stop treating that demand as a limiter. CTS reasoning is practical enough that a rehearsal with no review question can become unnecessary complexity.

## Training Load And Adaptation

This section explains how the CTS interpretation thinks about applying training stress and allowing adaptation to occur. It is concerned with the total pattern of work over time, not only the content of one workout. The central question is whether the runner is building a larger, more usable capacity while remaining able to absorb the next meaningful piece of training.

### Cumulative Workload Is A Primary Driver

The system gives substantial weight to the training an athlete can complete consistently across seasons and years. A single very long run, a dramatic high-volume week, or an impressive interval session does not replace accumulated work. Progress therefore favours patiently expanding the workload the runner can absorb, while avoiding enthusiasm-driven jumps that create more injury risk than useful adaptation. The desired outcome is not simply a larger training log; it is a runner who can complete useful work repeatedly, recover predictably, and arrive at specific preparation with more capacity than they had at the beginning of the cycle.

In this view, workload is not valuable because suffering through more training proves commitment. It is valuable because repeated exposure, followed by sufficient recovery, gradually changes the runner's ability to produce and sustain work. The coach therefore looks for a stable pattern: can the athlete complete a useful week, repeat it without accumulating unacceptable fatigue, and then progress one relevant part of it? If the answer is no, adding an exceptional session or a larger peak week may increase the appearance of training without increasing the capacity that matters.

This also explains why CTS gives the long-term training history real weight. A runner who has gradually built durable volume, movement tolerance, self-knowledge, and recovery habits may be ready for a more specific block that would overwhelm a runner with the same current fitness but little accumulated training. The principle is not that every athlete must chase high volume. It is that the workload must be earned, repeatable, and appropriate to the runner's event, life, and current stage of development.

### Volume Has Context

More volume can be useful, but its value depends on the runner's history, available time, recovery, and goal. CTS does not reduce workload to mileage alone. Time, frequency, long-run duration, vertical exposure, continuity, intensity, and training density all influence the work being performed. Cards should avoid implying that a distance or number of weekly hours is inherently superior without explaining the intended contribution. For a time-constrained athlete, for example, a longer individual run may create a different useful stimulus than the same time split into short outings; for another athlete, greater frequency may be the safer way to build workload. The profile keeps those as coaching choices rather than fixed rules.

The practical question is not "How much volume is ideal?" in isolation. It is "What form of volume develops the missing capacity with a cost this runner can recover from?" A runner preparing for a long mountain event may need some sustained time on feet and vertical exposure, while a newer runner may benefit more from frequent manageable runs than from forcing a long outing that disrupts the rest of the week. The same number on a training log can therefore represent very different preparation.

### Low Intensity Supports The Structure

Endurance running forms much of the usable workload and supports the ability to complete quality work, longer runs, and repeated weeks of training. A CTS interpretation should preserve the distinction between genuinely manageable endurance work and medium-hard running that quietly consumes recovery capacity without serving a clear purpose. This is not an argument that every easy session must be slow by an external standard; it is an argument that its effort must match its role. If a run is intended to support recovery or endurance volume, it should leave the runner able to complete the next planned work rather than turning into an accidental quality session.

The point is not to make low intensity passive or meaningless. Endurance work is where much of the total training load is accumulated and where the athlete practises efficient movement, pacing judgement, and basic durability. Its value is reduced when the runner repeatedly turns it into a test, because the plan then loses the contrast between work meant to build capacity quietly and work meant to create a more demanding stimulus.

### Quality Is Defined By Its Function

Steady work, tempo work, and shorter high-intensity intervals each serve different roles and require different dose, recovery, and placement. The profile should not flatten them into a generic category of "hard running." A card should describe the intended quality, the execution cue, the recovery arrangement, and why it belongs at that point in the plan. That explanation also gives the runner a way to adapt the session without losing it: terrain, pace, and interval format may change, but the intended effort and the reason for the recovery pattern should remain recognisable.

This level of definition protects both the athlete and the plan. It stops a runner from turning a steady aerobic workout into a threshold test, and it stops the coach from prescribing high-intensity work without a dose or recovery rationale. Quality is not judged only by how difficult the runner found it; it is judged by whether the correct stress was applied in a form that can be recovered from and used by the next stage of training.

### Block Work Is A Tool, Not A Badge

A focused block can improve a limiting quality by repeatedly exposing it over a defined period. It can also be inappropriate when the runner lacks the base, recovery capacity, or event context to absorb it. CTS-labelled cards should make the preconditions and recovery implications visible, particularly when they describe repeated high-intensity days, back-to-back long runs, or a sustained emphasis on one quality. The value lies in the accumulated exposure and the adaptation that follows, not in making a week look unusually difficult. A block with no protected recovery or no reason to concentrate the work is simply unmanaged density.

A useful block therefore needs a narrow question it is trying to answer. For example, can the runner improve a particular intensity range, add durable vertical work, or practise race-specific execution without losing the capacity to train the following week? If the answer cannot be described, the block is probably being used as a fashionable format rather than a targeted intervention.

The exit from a block matters as much as the entry. A focused period should end with enough recovery or transition space to reveal whether adaptation occurred. If the athlete leaves a block carrying unresolved fatigue, the coach may need to reduce the next layer rather than declare the block successful because it was completed.

### Strength Is Supportive, Not Automatic

CTS material supports well-designed strength work when it offers a useful return and does not compromise the central running plan. Some CTS material favours heavier, lower-repetition or power-oriented approaches for potential economy benefits, but it also recognises that expected performance gains may be modest and that strength work competes for time and recovery. This profile therefore does not treat strength as mandatory for every runner or prescribe a universal format. When strength is included, its role must be explicit: general robustness, force production, movement confidence, or a response to a known limitation. It should be progressed and reduced with the running load in mind, not added as a separate challenge that makes the core plan less executable.

This is deliberately more restrained than assuming that every trail runner needs a large strength programme. Strength may be highly worthwhile for one athlete and a lower priority for another whose limiting factor is simply consistent running time or recovery. CTS reasoning asks whether the strength work produces a practical return for this runner at this stage, and whether that return is greater than the running, recovery, or skill practice it displaces.

### Recovery Includes Life Outside Training

Training stress is only one part of the athlete's total load. Sleep, work, family responsibilities, emotional stress, and other life demands affect the capacity to adapt. A CTS plan should not use an apparently acceptable running workload as a reason to ignore an athlete whose overall recovery context has materially changed. The coaching response is not necessarily to abandon the whole plan; it may be to preserve the most important stimulus, reduce nonessential work, move a session, or take recovery before the accumulated stress turns a productive sequence into a setback.

This principle makes adaptation a coaching skill rather than a failure of discipline. The runner is not rewarded for completing a plan unchanged when the context has changed materially. The better outcome may be a smaller but well-executed week that preserves continuity and confidence, instead of a forced week that requires an extended recovery period and erodes the next phase of training.

## Trail And Mountain Application

This section explains how CTS's general endurance logic is adapted when the athlete runs on trails or prepares for mountainous ultramarathons. The aim is not to make all training trail-specific. It is to recognise when terrain changes the effort, the mechanical cost, the practical execution, or the race skill that must be developed.

### Prescribe The Work That The Terrain Allows

On trails, speed is often a poor common language because grade, surface, technicality, altitude, and descent can dramatically alter pace and cost. CTS frequently uses duration and rating of perceived exertion to make the intended workload and work rate practical across varied terrain. Pace, heart rate, power, grade-adjusted pace, and other data can still be informative, but they are interpreted rather than obeyed blindly. The coach should specify the training purpose first, then choose the most reliable way for that runner to feel and measure it on the available terrain. This makes a session transferable without pretending that all routes create the same experience.

This does not reject measurement; it puts measurement in the correct order. If the aim is a sustained climbing effort, the runner needs a route and cue that produce that effort, even if the pace is much slower than it would be on flat ground. If the athlete has no climb available, the coach can identify a substitute that preserves the main workload or movement demand, while being explicit about the part of the mountain experience that remains unpractised.

### Separate Cardiovascular Cost From Total Cost

Equivalent-pace calculations can be useful for trends, especially on reasonably comparable uphill terrain, but they do not fully capture technical surfaces or descending. The CTS interpretation recognises that foot speed, coordination, muscular damage, and neuromuscular fatigue can make a descent far more costly than its metabolic reading suggests. A plan should therefore account for descents in recovery and scheduling, not merely add elevation-loss totals to a spreadsheet. This distinction is important when evaluating why two runs with similar time, pace, or calculated load left the runner with very different legs. The answer may be in the terrain-specific mechanical and attentional demands that a simple model cannot see.

The broader lesson is that a model is only a partial description of the work. It may estimate the energetic cost well enough to compare two climbs but be poor at showing the mechanical cost of a fast technical descent. A CTS-informed coach uses the model to ask better questions, then checks it against how the athlete moved, felt, recovered, and performed in the following sessions.

### Use Specificity Selectively

Mountain and trail preparation should reflect the most consequential demands of the goal event, not copy every feature of the course in every session. For a runner with limited access, the coach identifies the capacity to develop and selects a practical substitute where one is reasonable. The profile remains honest when a substitute cannot reproduce technical movement, long descents, altitude, or environmental exposure. A runner may build climbing strength with a hill, stairs, or incline treadmill, for example, while still needing some real descent and technical-terrain exposure before a mountainous objective. Specificity is therefore a hierarchy of relevant demands, not a requirement to duplicate the whole race course every weekend.

This principle prevents two opposite errors. One is treating flatland training as useless for mountain races; the other is treating every available mountain feature as equally important. CTS-style specificity asks which abilities transfer well, which require real exposure, and how much direct practice is required before the event. The answer changes with the course, the runner's previous experience, and the risk created by getting that demand wrong.

### Build Descent Tolerance Without Chasing Damage

CTS material highlights the eccentric and neuromuscular cost of descending, while warning against assuming that punishing downhill sessions are automatically the answer. Regular, appropriately placed exposure may be more useful than a single destructive workout, especially near an event when recovery cost matters. A CTS card should distinguish the desired protective or technical stimulus from unnecessary soreness. The coach should also distinguish descent tolerance from descent skill: stronger quadriceps do not automatically create efficient foot placement, confidence, vision, or decision-making at speed on technical ground.

Descent preparation therefore needs progression and restraint. The runner may initially need exposure simply to learn how their legs respond, then later need more specific terrain or duration, but the progression should leave room for the rest of the plan. A downhill session that produces so much damage that it removes the next week's key work may be less specific in effect than a smaller repeated exposure that can be absorbed consistently.

### Rehearse Race-Execution Skills

Fueling, hydration, pacing, hiking where appropriate, equipment, heat or altitude strategy, and other race-day decisions are training skills as well as race-day choices. CTS views longer and more specific sessions as opportunities to practise and evaluate these systems. The runner should arrive with tested options rather than an unexamined plan. The review after a rehearsal matters as much as completing it: what was consumed, tolerated, forgotten, impractical, too slow, too fast, or unnecessarily complicated should inform the next attempt and eventually the race plan.

This makes rehearsal an experiment rather than a ritual. The runner should know what is being practised, capture the information needed to evaluate it, and change one or more variables deliberately next time. The aim is not to prove that a plan worked once in ideal conditions, but to reduce uncertainty about how the athlete will make decisions when fatigue, terrain, weather, and logistics interact on race day.

## Knowledge And Decision-Making

This section explains how a CTS-informed coach turns information into decisions. It covers the relationship between research, formal training concepts, athlete data, perceived effort, and real-world feedback. The purpose is to make guidance reasoned and reviewable without pretending that a dashboard, one study, or a completed workout can make the decision by itself.

### Use Science As A Starting Point

CTS presents its work as evidence-informed and frequently connects recommendations to research, but it also recognises that ultrarunning-specific evidence is incomplete and that findings from other endurance sports do not transfer perfectly. The correct interpretation is neither to reject research nor to treat every study as a final prescription. Research helps define plausible mechanisms, likely trade-offs, and useful boundaries; the coach still has to decide whether the participant group, intervention, terrain, event duration, and athlete context make that finding relevant here.

This stance is particularly important in trail and ultrarunning, where the event can be very different from the laboratory task or the sport from which a finding originates. A source may support using an intervention, but it does not automatically establish the exact dose, timing, or priority for every runner. The profile should preserve that difference between evidence of possibility and a confident individual prescription.

### Make The Reasoning Operational

The practical value of a training idea comes from turning it into a session, block, monitoring practice, or adjustment that a runner can execute and evaluate. CTS tends to favour clear definitions, measurable or observable training intent, and post-session interpretation over vague labels or fashionable complexity. A useful plan tells the runner what to do, how to recognise the appropriate effort, what to observe afterward, and what role the work plays in the larger sequence. It does not hide uncertainty behind a dashboard, a branded term, or a large number of inputs.

Operational does not mean mechanically rigid. A runner may need to slow, hike, shorten, move, or stop a session because the real-world conditions changed. The plan remains useful when the runner can identify the intended stimulus and make a sensible adjustment that preserves it, instead of viewing any deviation from the written numbers as failure.

### Use Data In Context

Watches, training platforms, workload scores, and physiological markers can help identify trends and compare similar work. They can also create false precision when surface, terrain, descent, accumulated fatigue, or data quality changes. A CTS interpretation uses data as an input to coaching judgement, paired with perceived effort, performance, recovery, and the runner's lived context. The same data point can be useful for one decision and misleading for another: a comparable uphill segment may reveal a trend, while a mixed technical route may be a poor basis for judging a runner's total recovery cost.

The coach should therefore ask what decision a metric is meant to inform before treating it as important. A number that cannot change a decision is often noise, while a simpler observation such as unusual difficulty at a familiar endurance effort may be more actionable. Data serves the training process when it improves interpretation, not when it replaces the runner's own report or creates a false sense of control.

### Learn From Response, Not Only From Compliance

Completing the written session is not the final measure of success. The coach should review whether the athlete received the intended stimulus, tolerated it, recovered from it, and remains on track for the larger plan. This makes the plan adjustable without becoming directionless. A session that needed a sensible modification can still be successful if its key purpose was preserved; a perfectly completed session can be a poor decision if it created disproportionate fatigue or displaced more important work later in the plan.

This creates a learning loop throughout the plan. The runner and coach can compare the planned stimulus with the actual response, then use that difference to improve future decisions. Over time, this produces a more individual understanding of what the athlete can absorb and what conditions make a particular type of work more or less useful.

CTS-labelled cards should therefore include the observation that makes the session reviewable. For a long run, that may be fueling, pacing, terrain tolerance, or recovery. For intervals, it may be execution quality and whether the surrounding easy work stayed easy enough. For downhill work, it may be delayed soreness and coordination. The card should help the runner collect the information that changes the next prescription.

## Implications For Cards

This section translates the CTS profile into standards for the training-card library. It does not prescribe a card schema or replace the shared authoring guidance. Instead, it defines the signs that a card has genuinely been shaped by CTS reasoning and therefore should carry the `cts` philosophy profile ID.

### Name The Performance Problem

A CTS-labelled card should name the event demand or adaptation it targets, not merely describe an attractive workout. The card should make clear whether it develops general workload capacity, an intensity-specific quality, climbing tolerance, descent durability, fueling practice, or another identified need.

Naming the problem gives the card a reason to exist and makes it easier to decide when it does not fit. A runner should be able to see whether the card addresses a real gap in their preparation or simply resembles a session they enjoy. This also prevents a card library from filling with superficially different workouts that all train the same quality.

### Give Terrain-Appropriate Execution Cues

A CTS-labelled card should explain the intended effort and execution cues in terrain-appropriate terms, usually prioritising time, RPE, movement, or controllable route features over a universal pace. When pace or a physiological metric is useful, it should serve the intended stimulus rather than override terrain, weather, or the runner's actual response.

The execution guidance should help the runner make a decision during the session, not only review it afterwards. They need to know what it should feel like, what variable can change safely, and what sign means the route or conditions are no longer allowing the intended work. This is especially important when a card will be used far from the terrain or coach that originally inspired it.

### Show The Card's Place In The Sequence

A CTS-labelled card should show prerequisite fitness, relationship to the larger training phase, recovery consequence, and what later work it prepares. This prevents users from treating a focused session or demanding long run as a stand-alone challenge detached from the workload that makes it productive.

The card does not need to become a full training plan, but it should make its dependencies visible. A reader should understand whether the session belongs after a period of general preparation, inside a focused block, near a race-specific phase, or as a lower-cost maintenance option. That context is what turns a workout description into a coaching tool.

### State The Level Of Use

A CTS-labelled card should distinguish foundational, specific, and advanced uses. Do not attach CTS to an advanced block, high-intensity session, or race rehearsal without stating who it is for, what preparation it assumes, and what signs suggest that the athlete should choose a simpler alternative.

This is not about creating artificial status tiers. It is about preventing a runner from selecting the most demanding-looking card before they have the capacity that makes it useful. A clear level of use also helps a coach progress an athlete from learning and general capacity toward more specific or concentrated work without presenting every option as equally appropriate.

### Offer Honest Substitutions

A CTS-labelled card should provide route or terrain substitutions when the core stimulus transfers, while naming any demand that cannot be reproduced fully. This makes the card useful to runners without ideal access while preserving the difference between, for example, building uphill work capacity and acquiring technical descending skill.

A substitution should preserve the main reason for the session, not merely offer any convenient activity. If a substitute changes the stimulus substantially, the card should say so and explain what it still develops. This lets runners adapt intelligently without being misled into believing that every indoor, road, or hill alternative creates the same preparation as the original terrain.

### Rehearse Only What Is Relevant

A CTS-labelled card should include fueling, downhill, environmental, or logistical rehearsal only when those are part of the session's real purpose. Adding every race-preparation element to every long run dilutes the signal; rehearsal works best when the runner knows what is being tested and how the result will influence the plan.

Focused rehearsal also makes review possible. If a run is meant to test fueling, the runner can assess intake, tolerance, timing, and practicality; if it is meant to develop endurance volume, a complex nutrition experiment may only distract from the main aim. The card should therefore distinguish essential rehearsal from optional practice and avoid treating all long runs as identical dress rehearsals.

### Use The Profile Deliberately

A card should not use the CTS profile merely because it contains intervals, a long run, a hill, strength work, or an ultramarathon reference. The card must reflect the system's demand-led, long-range, and cost-aware reasoning. Otherwise, the card should use a different philosophy that genuinely shaped its approach.

The philosophy label is provenance, not decoration. It tells a reader that the card's selection, progression, execution, and context were materially shaped by a particular coaching system. Using it sparingly makes future filtering and comparison meaningful; using it loosely would turn the label into a broad aesthetic category with no reliable coaching content.

## Current Limits And Review Questions

This section protects the profile from becoming more certain or complete than its source base supports. It records what still needs closer reading, where CTS material contains nuance or change over time, and what must be checked before a CTS interpretation is applied to existing or future cards.

- The source set is centred on publicly available CTS ultrarunning material and does not reproduce or replace *Training Essentials for Ultrarunning*, Second Edition. Add page-level notes from the book when we have reviewed the relevant passages.
- CTS material has evolved over time and includes different contexts, authors, and athlete populations. Treat the profile as a coherent interpretation of recurring themes, not as a claim that every CTS recommendation is permanent or universal.
- The exact role of strength training requires careful handling because CTS material frames its potential benefit, cost, and preferred implementation with important nuance. Keep generic cards conservative until a dedicated source review establishes the intended CTS position for each use case.
- Before assigning `cts` to existing cards, review each card against the implications above. A generic card should not carry the CTS label unless the CTS logic materially shaped its structure or guidance.
