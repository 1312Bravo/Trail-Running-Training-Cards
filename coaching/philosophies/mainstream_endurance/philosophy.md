# Mainstream Evidence-Informed Endurance Training

## Scope And Status

This file documents our interpretation of mainstream evidence-informed endurance training: the general exercise-science and coaching-education model commonly taught through university sport science, exercise physiology, strength and conditioning, and athletics coaching. It draws from institutional guidelines, academic textbooks, coaching education structures, consensus statements, and peer-reviewed reviews.

This is not an official document from ACSM, NSCA, World Athletics, USATF, Human Kinetics, or any university. It is not a branded coaching system. It should not be used to smuggle in the methods of CTS, Evoke Endurance, Some Work, All Play, Sharman Ultra, 80/20 Endurance, Lydiard, Daniels, Pfitzinger, or another named school. Those systems may overlap with mainstream principles, but they are not primary sources for this profile.

The profile is available for card authoring once reviewed. Its role is to describe the broad training logic that would be considered normal in an evidence-informed endurance curriculum: overload, specificity, progression, individualisation, recovery, variation, aerobic development, purposeful intensity, periodisation, tapering, and support work. Exact prescriptions, percentages, field tests, phase lengths, and workout formulas require source-specific review before being written into cards.

## Philosophy In Brief

Mainstream endurance training treats performance as the result of repeated, appropriately dosed training stress followed by sufficient recovery and adaptation. The coach manipulates frequency, duration, intensity, terrain, mode, strength load, and session density to create a stimulus the runner can absorb. The goal is not to complete the hardest possible session, but to accumulate useful work over time in a way that develops the capacities needed for the athlete's event and current stage.

The model begins from general training principles rather than from a signature workout. Specificity matters, but it is introduced in relation to prerequisites and timing. A runner first needs enough general aerobic capacity, musculoskeletal tolerance, movement skill, and training consistency to benefit from more demanding or event-like work. As the goal approaches, training becomes more similar to the demands that matter: duration, intensity, terrain, pacing, fatigue, fueling, environmental exposure, and technical movement.

This profile is deliberately plural. Mainstream teaching does not produce one exact endurance plan. Different textbooks, courses, sports, and coaches organise the same principles differently. The common thread is the decision logic: define the adaptation, choose a dose, progress gradually, preserve recovery, observe response, and adjust the next step.

## Endurance Performance Model

### Train The Determinants, Not The Vocabulary

Mainstream endurance teaching usually explains performance through interacting determinants rather than through one magic workout. For distance running, the usual model includes maximal aerobic capacity, the sustainable fraction of that capacity, lactate or ventilatory thresholds, running economy, substrate use, fatigue resistance, thermoregulation, musculoskeletal durability, skill, psychology, and event execution. A runner can improve by raising one determinant, by using an existing capacity more economically, or by losing less performance as duration and fatigue accumulate.

This matters for cards because a session should not be described only by its format. "Intervals" is not an adaptation. "Long run" is not an adaptation. The card should identify whether the work is developing aerobic capacity, threshold tolerance, economy, basic durability, specific endurance, neuromuscular coordination, heat tolerance, descending control, fueling practice, or some combination. The mainstream profile is strongest when it turns a training label into an adaptation hypothesis.

### Aerobic Capacity Is Necessary But Not Sufficient

Maximal oxygen uptake is an important endurance determinant, especially in shorter endurance events and in runners whose aerobic ceiling is underdeveloped. But mainstream teaching does not reduce performance to VO2max alone. Two runners with similar aerobic capacity can perform differently because of threshold location, economy, mechanical resilience, pacing, fueling, heat management, terrain skill, and the ability to maintain form late in the event.

The practical implication is that a card should not assume one limitation from one data point. A runner who fades late may need more aerobic volume, but may also need better pacing, more appropriate long-run progression, strength support, fueling practice, heat preparation, or less accumulated fatigue. The card should make the likely limiter explicit enough that the runner or coach can decide whether the card fits.

### Thresholds Describe Sustainable Work

Lactate threshold, ventilatory thresholds, critical speed, and related field estimates are common ways to describe transitions in the cost and sustainability of exercise. They help a coach separate easy aerobic work from controlled moderate work and from efforts that can only be sustained briefly. Different educational systems use different names and tests, but the mainstream principle is stable: intensity should be anchored to what the athlete can actually sustain, not to an abstract pace copied from another runner.

Cards should use threshold language carefully. A threshold estimate can guide session design, but it is not a complete assessment of readiness. Heat, fatigue, altitude, terrain, hydration, sleep, and measurement error can all change execution. When a card uses threshold-adjacent work, it should include effort and response cues so the runner can protect the intended stimulus when the metric is distorted.

### Economy Converts Fitness Into Speed Or Efficiency

Running economy describes the energy cost of running at a given speed or effort. It is influenced by biomechanics, stiffness, strength, coordination, footwear, surface, fatigue, and training history. Mainstream coaching gives economy a legitimate place because a runner can become better not only by enlarging the engine, but also by using less energy for the same task.

Economy work does not need to be complicated. Strides, relaxed faster running, hill form, strength training, plyometrics where appropriate, drills, and technical-terrain skill can all support more economical movement. The useful version is specific enough to transfer and controlled enough to preserve quality. If the runner is too fatigued to move well, the economy stimulus has probably been lost.

### Durability And Fatigue Resistance Are Practical Outcomes

Mainstream literature often discusses fatigue through physiological mechanisms, but the coaching problem is practical: can the runner keep producing the required movement after duration, load, terrain, heat, fueling demands, and accumulated stress? Durability is the ability to tolerate and repeat training. Fatigue resistance is the ability to preserve performance or movement quality as fatigue rises.

These ideas should not become a licence for exhaustion. A session can investigate or develop late-run durability without asking the runner to collapse. The card should identify which feature of fatigue is being trained: longer aerobic duration, strength under repeated impact, downhill tolerance, fueling under effort, pacing judgement, or economy after accumulated running. That keeps the concept useful and proportionate.

### Health And Availability Are Performance Determinants

In mainstream coaching, a runner who cannot train consistently is not merely unlucky; availability itself is part of performance preparation. Sleep, energy availability, illness, injury history, psychological load, work stress, and life disruption change the effective dose of training. The best plan on paper is not best in practice if it repeatedly creates missed training, persistent soreness, low motivation, or unresolved health concerns.

This does not mean avoiding demanding work. It means demanding work must be earned, placed, and recovered from. Cards should treat consistency, readiness, and health boundaries as part of the training model, not as a disclaimer attached after the real coaching has happened.

## Coaching Principles

### Start With Training Principles, Not A Signature Method

The mainstream model is built from general principles of training. Specificity asks whether the work resembles the capacity or performance demand being developed. Overload asks whether the dose is sufficient to stimulate adaptation. Progression asks whether the runner is prepared for a larger or more complex dose. Individualisation asks whether the prescription fits this athlete, not only the event category. Variation and periodisation organise stress so that the athlete can continue adapting without every week carrying the same demand.

These principles are practical filters. A session is not good because it has a famous name or because it looks difficult. It is good when it applies the right stress at the right time for a clear reason. The same hill repeat, tempo run, or long run can be appropriate, premature, misplaced, or irrelevant depending on the runner's history, current phase, recovery, and goal demands.

### Build Aerobic Capacity As A Central Resource

For endurance running, aerobic capacity and aerobic durability are central. Most runners need a substantial amount of repeatable low-to-moderate stress to improve the cardiovascular, metabolic, muscular, and connective-tissue systems that support longer performance. This does not mean every run is slow, nor that all athletes need high mileage. It means that general endurance capacity is usually the platform from which more specific intensity, race preparation, and terrain demands become useful.

The coach should distinguish aerobic development from simply collecting distance. Frequency, total time, surface, vertical gain, mechanical load, and recovery response all change what a given volume means. A runner who cannot repeat a week without excessive soreness, poor sleep, or declining movement quality is not building a stronger base simply because the log is larger.

### Use Intensity Deliberately

Mainstream endurance training recognises a range of useful intensities: recovery, easy aerobic, steady, threshold-adjacent, high-intensity interval work, neuromuscular speed, hills, and race-specific efforts. The principle is not that one intensity is always superior. The principle is that each intensity has a job, a cost, and a place in the larger pattern.

A common coaching error is accidental intensity: easy runs drift upward, long runs become tests, and moderate-hard effort appears too often because it feels productive. The mainstream response is not to remove all harder work. It is to make intensity explicit. The card should say what quality is being trained, how the runner should recognise it, and what recovery or surrounding easy work protects it.

### Individualise From The General Model

Academic and coaching-education models provide starting points, not automatic prescriptions. Two athletes preparing for the same event may need different progressions because their training history, injury background, age, life stress, terrain access, strengths, weaknesses, and recovery capacity differ. Individualisation is not random preference; it is disciplined adjustment of a principle to the actual runner.

This profile should therefore avoid falsely precise claims. A guideline can describe a reasonable dose or progression pattern, but the card must preserve the conditions that make it appropriate. A new runner, a returning runner, and a highly trained runner can all use mainstream principles while needing very different sessions.

### Make Specificity Progressive

Specificity improves transfer when the athlete is ready for it. In running, that may mean race-relevant duration, surface, gradient, intensity, heat, altitude, fueling, equipment, descent, technical terrain, or pacing. The closer training comes to the event, the more carefully the coach must judge both benefit and cost.

The mainstream model does not require early race simulation. General capacity, basic strength, aerobic durability, and movement quality often need to come first. Specificity then becomes a progression: introduce a relevant demand, observe the response, and increase resemblance to the goal only when the runner can absorb it.

### Treat Skill And Mechanics As Trainable

Endurance is not only metabolism. Running economy, coordination, stride rhythm, hill technique, descending control, hiking transitions, balance, and movement confidence all influence how efficiently a runner uses fitness. Mainstream training should give these qualities a place without turning every session into a technical drill.

Skill work is most useful when it is specific enough to transfer and simple enough to execute well. Strides, drills, relaxed accelerations, hill form work, basic strength, mobility, and controlled terrain practice can all support the endurance plan when they are placed with attention to fatigue and learning quality.

### Keep The Runner's Developmental Stage Visible

Mainstream coaching changes the training question according to developmental stage. A beginner may need frequency, basic movement tolerance, confidence, and simple aerobic consistency. A returning runner may need reconditioning and protection from the memory of former fitness. An intermediate runner may need better distribution, long-run development, or a clearer progression. An advanced runner may need more precise manipulation of intensity, specificity, recovery, and small performance determinants.

The profile should therefore avoid writing cards as if every runner is a smaller version of an elite athlete. The same principle can apply across levels, but the dose and priority change. A card should state who it fits, what preparation it assumes, and what simpler version preserves the main purpose.

### Separate General Preparation From Specific Preparation

Mainstream training theory commonly distinguishes general preparation from more specific preparation. General preparation develops capacities that support many later options: aerobic base, movement quality, strength, mobility, consistency, and broad durability. Specific preparation narrows the work toward the demands of the target event.

This distinction helps prevent both impatience and vagueness. A runner should not rush into race-like work before the foundation can support it, but they also should not remain forever in general training when the goal requires specific demands. Cards should name whether they are building broad capacity, converting capacity, rehearsing event demands, or reducing fatigue before performance.

## Training Load And Adaptation

### Dose Stress So It Can Be Absorbed

Training adaptation depends on the relationship between stress and recovery. Overload must be large enough to stimulate change, but not so large or dense that the athlete cannot restore function, maintain consistency, or complete later key work. A hard session that disrupts the next week may be less productive than a smaller dose that can be repeated and progressed.

Cards shaped by this profile should therefore describe both the intended stimulus and the expected recovery implication. The question is not only "Can the athlete do this today?" It is also "Can they absorb it and continue the training process?"

### Progress One Meaningful Variable At A Time

Progression can come from more frequency, longer duration, greater weekly volume, more vertical gain, higher intensity, shorter recovery, more technical terrain, added strength load, or greater session density. These variables interact. Increasing several at once can hide the true dose and make it difficult to understand the athlete's response.

A mainstream-informed card should identify the variable that should progress first and the variable that should remain stable. For example, a long-run card may progress duration while keeping effort easy; a hill card may increase repetitions before technicality; a strength-support card may increase load before complexity. This makes progression reviewable rather than simply larger.

### Vary Training To Maintain Adaptation And Recovery

Variation is not random variety. It is the planned alternation of stress and relief across sessions, microcycles, blocks, and seasons. Easier days, reduced-load weeks, changes in terrain, cross-training, and different session types can all help the runner continue adapting while managing monotony and overuse risk.

The correct amount of variation depends on the athlete and phase. Too little variation can create repetitive strain or stagnation. Too much can prevent a clear stimulus from accumulating. The card library should use variation to support the training purpose, not to make every week look novel.

### Use Periodisation As A Planning Tool

Mainstream coaching education commonly teaches training organisation through sessions, microcycles, mesocycles, macrocycles, annual plans, and multi-year development. The point is not the terminology itself. The point is that training stress, support work, specificity, competition, and recovery need a coherent order.

Periodisation can be linear, block-oriented, mixed, or flexible. This profile should not claim one model is always best. A card should instead make its place in the larger plan visible: foundation, development, specific preparation, integration, taper, recovery, or return to consistency.

### Taper To Express Fitness, Not To Create It

Tapering reduces accumulated fatigue while preserving enough training intensity and rhythm for performance readiness. The mainstream evidence supports tapering as a real performance tool, but exact duration and load reduction depend on event, athlete, preceding load, and recovery pattern.

Cards should treat taper sessions as reminders or maintenance, not last-minute fitness builders. A taper card should reduce volume and cost, preserve confidence and movement quality, and avoid unfamiliar demands that create soreness or uncertainty.

### Include Strength As Supportive Training

Strength training can support running through force production, movement quality, tendon and muscle capacity, running economy, resilience, and general health. It is not automatically the highest priority, and it must be placed so it does not undermine the primary endurance work.

The mainstream stance is supportive and contextual. Strength work should have a reason, an entry point, and a progression. Heavy resistance, plyometrics, basic control, mobility, and muscular endurance methods are not interchangeable; the card should state which quality is being targeted and how it interacts with the running load.

### Build Long-Term Capacity Across Cycles

Mainstream endurance development is cumulative. The runner is not built only by the current week, but by repeated cycles of training, recovery, consolidation, and renewed progression. Good training leaves behind capacities that can support the next cycle: better aerobic fitness, improved movement economy, more durable tissues, stronger habits, clearer self-knowledge, and a larger margin before specialised work becomes costly.

This gives the card library a useful standard: a card should not encourage an isolated performance display unless that is truly the point of the phase. Most cards should help the runner become more trainable, more consistent, or more prepared for the next layer. A session that looks impressive but makes the following work worse is suspect under this profile.

### Use Recovery Weeks And Consolidation Deliberately

Reduced-load periods are a normal part of mainstream training, not evidence that progress has stopped. They can lower fatigue, allow adaptation to appear, reduce monotony, and give the coach clearer feedback about whether the previous work was productive. Consolidation may mean holding volume steady, reducing intensity, repeating a manageable week, or temporarily shifting toward easier movement.

Cards should avoid treating every week as a required escalation. A runner can progress by completing similar work with lower cost, recovering faster, moving better late in the session, or becoming more consistent. That is especially important after illness, travel, injury interruption, or a demanding terrain block.

### Manage Monotony, Density, And Load Spikes

Training load is shaped by more than total distance or hours. Monotony, sudden increases, dense scheduling, hard sessions placed too close together, unfamiliar surfaces, heavy strength work, travel, and psychological stress can all change risk and adaptation. A plan can look reasonable in weekly totals while being poorly arranged day to day.

The mainstream response is to look at the pattern. Which sessions carry the most cost? Where is the recovery? Which variables changed at the same time? What did the athlete report after the work? A card should make its expected cost visible so it can be placed intelligently, especially when it includes intensity, long duration, descent, strength, heat, or technical terrain.

### Respect Energy Availability And Basic Recovery Behaviours

Endurance training depends on the athlete having enough energy and recovery capacity to adapt. Underfueling, poor sleep, accumulated stress, illness, and repeated training through warning signs can turn a sensible plan into a non-productive one. This profile should not prescribe clinical nutrition or sleep treatment, but it should recognise that the training dose includes the athlete's ability to support the work.

For cards, the practical standard is simple: long, intense, or high-cost sessions should include enough execution context that the runner does not treat completion as the only task. Fueling practice, hydration, sleep, easy-day discipline, and recovery spacing may be part of making the session trainable.

### Place Cross-Training And Alternative Modes With A Purpose

Mainstream endurance coaching often uses cross-training or alternative modes to preserve aerobic stimulus, reduce impact, maintain frequency, or manage return-to-run progression. Cycling, hiking, uphill walking, elliptical work, pool running, skiing, and strength circuits can each be useful when the substitution preserves the intended adaptation.

The limitation is transfer. A low-impact aerobic substitute may support cardiovascular development but may not build the same running-specific tissue tolerance, downhill control, foot placement, or race execution. A card should say whether the alternative is equivalent for the purpose, partially useful, or only a temporary bridge.

## Intensity And Session Design

### Use Intensity Domains As Tools

Mainstream teaching often separates exercise intensity into domains or zones using ventilatory thresholds, lactate response, heart rate, pace, power, RPE, or performance tests. The exact model can vary: three-zone, five-zone, seven-zone, pace-based, heart-rate-based, power-based, or RPE-based systems can all be used responsibly. The important point is that intensity categories help the coach prescribe, execute, and review training.

This profile should not claim that one zone system is mandatory. A card should choose the simplest intensity language that protects the session purpose. For many trail sessions, RPE, breathing, and movement quality may be more robust than pace. For controlled road or track work, pace or split times may be useful. For longer aerobic work, heart rate can help but must be interpreted in context.

### Distinguish Easy, Moderate, Hard, And Fast

Easy work supports aerobic development and recovery capacity. Moderate work can develop sustained output or race-adjacent tolerance, but it carries more cost than many runners expect. Hard interval work targets higher aerobic power, threshold stress, or race-specific demands. Fast neuromuscular work can train coordination and mechanics without necessarily creating a large metabolic load.

These categories should not be blurred. A short stride is fast but not a hard interval session. A hilly long run can be easy by breathing but hard by muscular cost. A steady run can be useful, but if it appears too often it may quietly consume recovery. Cards should make the distinction clear enough that the runner does not accidentally turn every session into the same middle effort.

### Design Sessions Around Warm-Up, Main Stimulus, Recovery, And Cool-Down

Mainstream session design usually considers preparation, the main training stimulus, recovery intervals or recovery days, and the return to easy movement. Warm-ups are not decorative; they prepare the cardiovascular, muscular, neural, and technical systems for the work. Recoveries are not laziness; they shape the stimulus. Cool-downs and post-session reflection help transition out of the work and evaluate the response.

For session cards, the workout structure should therefore explain why the recoveries exist and what quality they protect. Short recoveries, long recoveries, walking recoveries, and full easy days all create different training effects. The runner should understand enough to adapt the format without losing the purpose.

### Use Long Runs As Specific Endurance Tools

The long run is a mainstream endurance tool because it extends duration, develops aerobic endurance, practises fueling where relevant, exposes the musculoskeletal system to longer loading, and builds confidence with sustained movement. But long runs are not automatically better when made harder, longer, or more race-like.

A long-run card should state whether the purpose is basic aerobic duration, time on feet, event-specific terrain, fueling practice, progression under fatigue, hiking, descent tolerance, or psychological familiarity with duration. Those are different sessions. Treating them as one generic "long run" makes progression and recovery harder to manage.

### Use Competition And Tune-Up Events Carefully

Races, time trials, and tune-up events can provide feedback, motivation, pacing practice, and specific intensity. They also add cost and can distort training if every event becomes a maximal performance priority. Mainstream coaching treats competition as part of the training plan, not as an interruption floating outside it.

Cards do not need to prescribe races directly, but race-practice or assessment cards should make the purpose clear. Is the runner testing fitness, rehearsing pacing, practising fueling, learning terrain, or sharpening before a goal? The answer changes how hard the effort should be and how much recovery it deserves.

## Trail And Mountain Application

### Translate The Principles To Terrain

Trail and mountain running do not invalidate mainstream endurance principles. They change how those principles are applied. Pace becomes less reliable when gradient, surface, altitude, footing, and descent vary. Time, perceived exertion, heart rate, power, vertical gain, movement quality, and recovery response may each be useful depending on the session.

The card should preserve the intended adaptation rather than force a road-derived metric onto terrain that changes the cost. An easy aerobic run may require hiking on climbs. A threshold-like session may need smoother terrain. A long technical outing may create substantial mechanical fatigue even when cardiovascular intensity stays modest.

### Account For Mechanical And Technical Cost

Trail training can add eccentric loading, coordination demand, attentional fatigue, stabilising work, and muscular damage that are not fully captured by pace or heart rate. Downhill running is especially important because it may feel aerobically easy while creating delayed soreness and reduced movement quality.

Mainstream load management should therefore include terrain as a dose variable. A card should specify whether the terrain is central to the stimulus or incidental. If the runner needs the aerobic stimulus without the full terrain cost, the card can offer flatter, smoother, treadmill, hiking, or cross-training alternatives.

### Use Specificity In Proportion To The Goal

Specificity should match the consequential demands of the event. A runnable trail race, a steep mountain ultra, a technical descent-heavy course, and a hot exposed event require different preparation. Mainstream training does not require every run to be trail-specific; it requires the plan to prepare the athlete for the demands that matter.

The coach should decide which demands transfer from general running and which require direct exposure. Aerobic fitness may transfer well. Technical descending, pole use, night movement, heat strategy, fueling, and long hiking may need explicit practice. The card should name that distinction.

### Preserve Access-Aware Substitutions

A runner without mountains can still develop useful endurance capacity, strength, climbing movement, and pacing discipline. Hills, stairs, treadmill incline, strength work, hiking, and route selection can substitute for some training purposes. They cannot fully replace technical footing, long descents, altitude, weather exposure, or the psychological and logistical demands of remote terrain.

This profile should make substitutions practical and honest. The runner should understand what the alternative preserves and what it leaves unpractised.

### Prepare Environmental Stress Progressively

Heat, cold, altitude, wind, rain, mud, snow, darkness, and remoteness can change both performance and safety. Mainstream exercise physiology gives environmental preparation a real place because conditions alter thermoregulation, hydration needs, perceived effort, energy cost, pacing, and risk. These demands should be introduced progressively and only when relevant to the goal.

A card should avoid treating environmental difficulty as character training. The purpose may be heat acclimation, equipment testing, pacing under warmer conditions, confidence in poor footing, or learning hydration needs. If conditions create unsafe exposure or change the session beyond its purpose, the correct coaching decision may be to alter the route, reduce duration, move the session, or choose a safer substitute.

### Treat Fueling And Hydration As Trainable Execution

For longer endurance events, fueling and hydration are not only race-day details. They affect whether training can be completed, recovered from, and translated into performance. Mainstream guidance supports practising nutrition and hydration strategies in relevant sessions while keeping clinical nutrition advice outside the card's scope.

The practical card standard is to include fueling or hydration only when it matters to the session purpose. A short easy run does not need complex instruction. A long run, heat session, race rehearsal, or high-cost mountain outing may need simple prompts about practising intake, timing, tolerance, and post-session review.

### Include Safety And Route Judgement In The Dose

Trail and mountain sessions carry route, weather, footing, remoteness, navigation, daylight, and equipment considerations. These are not separate from training. They affect whether the session can be executed as intended and whether the risk is proportionate to the benefit.

Mainstream coaching does not need to make every card a safety manual, but a terrain-specific card should include the decision points that change the session. If the route forces higher effort, unsafe descending, poor visibility, or exposure beyond the athlete's skill, the session has become a different dose.

## Knowledge And Decision-Making

### Use Evidence As A Decision Aid

Mainstream endurance training is evidence-informed, not evidence-pretending. Research, textbooks, guidelines, and coaching education provide mechanisms, ranges, and likely trade-offs. They do not automatically determine the correct session for an individual runner on a given day.

Cards should therefore connect claims to a decision. A source may support that tapering can improve performance, that strength training can improve running economy, or that excessive load without recovery increases risk. The card still needs to translate that into a practical choice: when to use it, how to dose it, what to monitor, and when to simplify.

### Combine External Metrics With Internal Response

Mainstream practice uses heart rate, pace, power, lactate, rating of perceived exertion, training load, performance tests, and athlete-reported response. Each measure is partial. Pace can fail on trails. Heart rate can drift with heat or fatigue. Load scores can miss technical difficulty. Perceived effort can be influenced by mood, sleep, motivation, or environment.

The coach should choose the metric that best protects the intended stimulus, then check it against how the runner moved, felt, recovered, and performed later. A card should teach the runner what matters most for that session rather than presenting data as a verdict.

### Respect Uncertainty And Scope

The mainstream profile should be careful with medical, nutritional, psychological, and injury claims. Training cards can encourage sensible progression, recovery awareness, and professional support when needed. They should not diagnose injury, prescribe clinical treatment, or promise risk elimination.

Where evidence is mixed or context-dependent, the card should use proportionate language. It is acceptable to say that a method is commonly supported, plausible, or useful for many runners, while still naming the conditions that affect its value.

### Test To Inform The Next Decision

Testing and assessment are common in mainstream endurance coaching: lab tests, field time trials, threshold estimates, heart-rate drift checks, submaximal benchmarks, strength screens, movement observations, training logs, race results, and subjective wellness data can all be useful. The purpose is to improve the next decision, not to create a permanent label for the athlete.

A test should match the question. If the coach needs to set training intensity, a controlled field test may help. If the question is readiness after illness, an easy run response may be more useful than a maximal effort. If the question is trail durability, a flat road time trial may be only partial evidence. Cards that include assessment should state what the result can and cannot tell us.

### Prefer Repeatable Measures Over Impressive Measures

A useful measurement is one that can be repeated with enough consistency to inform change. Familiar routes, similar conditions, standardised warm-ups, comparable terrain, and clear perceived-effort notes often matter more than sophisticated data collected in inconsistent contexts. The mainstream profile should respect both technology and simplicity.

For a card library, this means assessment language should be humble. A watch metric, lab number, segment time, or subjective score can guide training, but only if the coach understands the conditions around it. A single result should rarely override the broader pattern of training response.

### Review The Response, Not Only The Completion

Mainstream coaching closes the loop after training. The runner completed the session, but did it produce the intended stimulus? Was pacing controlled? Did movement quality hold? Did the runner recover as expected? Did soreness, mood, sleep, or ordinary running effort change afterward? This feedback determines whether the next step should progress, repeat, simplify, or shift focus.

Cards should encourage this learning loop in practical terms. The runner does not need a long report after every easy run, but key sessions, new terrain stress, long runs, and assessment workouts should create useful information.

## Implications For Cards

### Use The Label For General Training Logic

Use `mainstream_endurance` when a card is materially shaped by broad sport-science or coaching-education principles, and no named coaching profile is doing the distinctive work. Good examples include general aerobic development, progressive workload growth, recovery-oriented adaptation, general periodisation, tapering, strength support, and basic specificity.

Do not use this label merely because a card is normal, sensible, or broadly useful. The card should show the mainstream logic in its fields: intended adaptation, progression variable, recovery relationship, placement in the plan, and practical execution cues.

### Keep `common` Separate

`common` remains the shared coaching foundation: athlete-centred tone, professional scope, durable development, trail-aware judgement, access awareness, and safety boundaries. `mainstream_endurance` is different. It describes the general training-method model.

If a card only follows the shared foundation without making a specific training-method claim, it should remain `common`. If it uses general endurance science to structure progression, adaptation, periodisation, or specificity, `mainstream_endurance` may be appropriate.

### Avoid Named-System Drift

This profile must not become a quiet duplicate of 80/20, Lydiard, CTS, Evoke, SWAP, or Sharman Ultra. If a card depends on a low-intensity-heavy distribution as a distinctive organizing principle, use `80_20_endurance`. If it depends on base-first sequential timing and inner-coach response in the Lydiard sense, use `lydiard`. If it depends on mountain layered capacity and muscular endurance, use `evoke_endurance`. If it depends on event-demand-led ultrarunning specificity, use `cts`. If it depends on SWAP's economy, speed, fulfilment, and fatigue-resistance framing, use `swap`. If it depends on Sharman Ultra's adaptive ultra-execution coaching, use `sharman_ultra`.

`mainstream_endurance` should be the neutral academic baseline, not the label for every good idea.

### Make Progression And Recovery Visible

A mainstream-labelled card should state how the work progresses, what should stay controlled, and what response indicates that the next dose should be reduced or delayed. It should avoid implying that progress always means more.

The card should also describe recovery cost in the same practical language as the stimulus. A session's training value depends on whether it can be absorbed and integrated into the surrounding plan.

### Make Specificity Transferable

When a card includes terrain, speed, long duration, strength, or race-like work, it should say what specific quality is being developed and what can be substituted. This is especially important for trail runners, because the same route can train aerobic endurance, muscular endurance, downhill skill, technical coordination, or race execution depending on how it is used.

### State The Performance Determinant

A mainstream-labelled card should name the determinant or capability it is trying to influence: aerobic capacity, threshold control, economy, durability, specific endurance, strength support, movement skill, heat tolerance, recovery, or race execution. If the determinant cannot be named, the card may be too vague or may belong only to `common`.

### Include Assessment Boundaries When Relevant

If a card uses testing, benchmarks, zones, or metrics, it should say what decision the information supports. It should also name the limits of the measure. This prevents field tests, watch data, and race results from being treated as more precise than they are.

### Protect Beginner And Returning Runner Use

Cards under this profile should avoid assuming advanced readiness unless that is explicit. A mainstream card can be rigorous and still provide a basic entry point. For beginner, returning, older, injury-prone, or time-limited runners, the correct mainstream version may be lower frequency, shorter duration, more walking, lower technicality, more recovery spacing, or a longer build.
